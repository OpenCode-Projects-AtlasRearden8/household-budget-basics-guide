# The Bills Calendar

A bills calendar is one page — paper or notes-app — that lists every recurring bill by **the day it's due**, every month, all year. That's it. It's the single highest-leverage half hour in household money management, because due dates are the main ambush in everyday finances.

## Why this works

Money trouble for most households is rarely "we can't afford anything." It's usually "four things hit in the same week and we thought we had three more weeks." The bills calendar makes you see that pile-up months in advance, while it's still free to fix.

## Build it in one sitting (about 30 minutes)

1. **Gather the sources.** Bank and card statements (last 2 months), email inbox ("receipt", "invoice", "auto-pay"), and walls/drawers full of paper bills. Grab the [Bills Calendar worksheet](08-worksheets.html).
2. **Walk a normal month.** For each bill, write: name, due day-of-month, amount (or a typical range), and how it gets paid (auto-pay, app, mail-in).
3. **Sort by day** — 1st through 31st, then the annuals (property tax, car registration) in their own little section or noted at their months.
4. **Color-code or star** anything that changes month to month (electric, water) vs. fixed (internet, insurance).

That's the whole build. No app required, though you can absolutely keep this page alive in a shared note or on a fridge calendar. Shared with your partner/roommates, it ends the "oh, *that* bill" conversation forever.

## Use it weekly

This is literally what the [weekly ten-minute money date](07-money-date.html) does with the calendar:

- Look 7–10 days ahead. What's due before the next date?
- Confirm each upcoming bill is funded. Any gaps are exactly what your [buffer](01-paper-first-month.html) and [sinking funds](03-sinking-funds.html) exist for.
- If a bill you can't fully cover is upcoming, you know today — not on the due date.

## The alignment move (optional but powerful)

When you build the calendar, you'll likely notice a bunch of bills due the first week of the month and only a paycheck part-covering them. Most companies will shift a due date — insurance, utilities, and card companies all do this. Ask: "Can we move my due date to the 18th?" Companies do this more often than you'd think, and they never bring it up unless you do.

Aim for a rhythm where your biggest bills all sit within a few days **after** your paycheck dates. Two five-minute phone calls can restructure a whole month.

## The annual layer

Your calendar should have a page-two: the once-a-year heavies. Car registration, property taxes, holiday season, kids' camps. Each one gets claimed by a [sinking fund](03-sinking-funds.html) — the calendar tells you the due date and total, and the sinking fund collects for it all year in tiny slices.

## When a bill lands wrong

Life happens to the best calendar:

- **Caught it before the due date:** the calendar did its job. Cover from the buffer, or call the company — many will shift a date or offer a short extension with no drama, especially if you call *before* rather than after.
- **Already late:** don't spiral. Pay what you can, call, ask about a fee waiving. One late fee is a data point. The *Credit & Debt Calm Guide* has the full playbook for worse scenarios (companion project).
- **A bill simply doesn't happen** (a subscription quietly canceled itself): correct the calendar, and celebrate with the [splurge line](05-splurge-line.html).

## The trick: no guilt, just maintenance

Bills Calendars succeed when they're boring. Print one, or one shared digital note, and treat it like the family wall calendar — no mood, no shame, just "what's due next." It's the closest thing in personal finance to a free lunch.

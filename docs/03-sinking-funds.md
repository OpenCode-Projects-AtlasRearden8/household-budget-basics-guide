# Sinking Funds: The End of "Surprise" Bills

Here's a friendly truth: after your first year of adult life, essentially nothing that hits your bank account is *actually* a surprise. Car registration comes every twelve months. Winter jackets come every winter. The dog goes to the vet. Christmas lands on December 25th, annually, identical date, like clockwork.

A **sinking fund** is a simple system for inviting those bills to the table on your terms. You pick the thing, you divide its cost by the months until it's due, and you set that small amount aside each month. When the bill arrives, it's just... already handled. That's the whole idea, and it works stupidly well.

## Why not just "save money"?

When you "save money" generically, the savings account is a pool with no names on it. Every bill that hits it feels like it *stole* money from "the future." With a sinking fund, the car-repair fund is *supposed* to be spent on the car. Using it feels like a win, not a defeat. The psychology matters more than the math.

## Build your first three funds

Start small. Three funds, well-fed, beat ten funds that all get $3.

**Fund 1 — Car & transport.** Registration, inspections, tires, oil changes, that one mystery rattle. Estimate your year's real cost (look back at last year, round up 25%), divide by 12. $600/year = $50/month.

**Fund 2 — Health & pets.** Co-pays, contacts, dental cleanings, vet visits, meds that "you only sometimes need." For households with pets or chronic-anything, this is the fund that prevents the most panic.

**Fund 3 — Gift card or holiday fund.** Holiday season, birthdays, teacher gifts. Divide last December's pain by 12. December becomes a scheduled month instead of a mugging.

Add more only when these three are running on autopilot: home/car repair beyond the first fund, kids' activities, travel, and "the celebration line" for the good things your family actually celebrates.

## The lazy math (encouraged)

You do not need precise math, you need *round* math:

- Last year's cost: $730 → round to $750 or $800 → ÷ 12 → $65/month. Done.
- Unknown cost (vet bills): pick a number that feels non-terrifying: $40/month. It compounds, and the guess gets calibrated next year.

Fill this in on the [Sinking Funds worksheet](08-worksheets.html). If a fund comes up empty the first time it's used, that's calibration, not failure — adjust the monthly slice and move on.

## Where to keep the money

Two good options and one to avoid:

- **Simple version:** one savings account, and a paper note (or spreadsheet) listing what each chunk is for. Totally fine. The *label* is the fund.
- **Bank-organized version:** most credit unions and banks support named savings "buckets" or multiple savings accounts. One account per fund, nicknames like "dog & doctor."
- **Skip:** high-pressure "savings apps" that pull money you didn't plan. A fund you control beats an app you debug.

If your plan uses cash-style categories — which is the whole idea behind the companion **Envelope Budget Webapp** — sinking funds are just envelopes with long horizons. Same muscle, longer game.

## Rules that keep funds kind

1. **Borrowing from a fund is allowed.** Cover a true surprise by borrowing from the *least* urgent fund, then note the loan and repay it on the next money date. Ruthless clean books are what wreck systems; readable loans are what keep them alive.
2. **Refill immediately after use.** The day you spend from a fund, set its next refill plan. Ten seconds on the [weekly date](07-money-date.html).
3. **Close funds you're done with.** Fund finished its job? Move the remainder to buffer or your next big thing. Wins should be felt.
4. **Never hide a fund from your partner.** The list lives on one shared page — this tool is for togetherness, not secrecy.

## The quiet payoff

The first time your car needs $450 of brakes and you pay it with a shrug because "the car fund was for this," you'll understand why this is one of most people's favorite tools in the whole guide. It's not about restriction; it's about *pre-paying your own calm*.
